1 · The big idea

The website goes with the paper frise: the frise gives the dates, the site goes deeper with experiments, animations, videos and sources. It's an interdisciplinary project: SVT, Physique-Chimie, NSI and Arts.

Everything uses the same colour code as the frise:

  • bleu Innovation technique: a new tool (microscope, bio-printer…)
  • rose Découverte scientifique: a new idea about nature (the cell, the nucleus, the expanding Universe…)

3 · « Le fil du temps »: the interactive frise

The same 23 events as the paper frise, grouped into 5 eras:

  1. Les origines: Big Bang, Démocrite
  2. Les premiers microscopes (1590–1674): Janssen, Hooke, Leeuwenhoek
  3. Atomes, éléments, cellules (1789–1869): Lavoisier, Dalton, Brown, Schleiden & Schwann, Virchow, Mendeleïev
  4. Le noyau et l'Univers (1896–1931): Becquerel, Thomson, the Curies, Rutherford, Lemaître, Hubble, Ruska
  5. Voir les atomes, aller dans l'espace (1953–2023): Franklin (DNA), Penzias & Wilson, the scanning tunnelling microscope, Pesquet, Al Neyadi

How it works: on a laptop, scrolling down (or swiping sideways on the trackpad) moves through time horizontally; on a phone it becomes a vertical story. Click any card, dot or era to open a fact sheet: photos, a longer explanation, a “Le saviez-vous ?”, its sources and a link to the chapter.

5 · Chapter 1 · Les premiers regards dans l'invisible

Hooke (1665) looks at cork with a microscope, sees little boxes and names them “cellules” (Latin cella, small room). Leeuwenhoek (1674), with a single-lens microscope (~270×), is the first to see bacteria and micro-organisms, his “animalcules”.

Experiment: redo Hooke's cork observation. Animation: a drop of pond water, what Leeuwenhoek saw.

Every chapter has the same structure: L'essentiel → key fact → experiment → photos → animation → videos → sources.

7 · Chapter 3 · La radioactivité

Becquerel (1896): uranium salts darken a photographic plate wrapped in black paper, so uranium gives off invisible radiation. Marie & Pierre Curie (1898) discover polonium and radium. Marie Curie is the only person with Nobel prizes in two different sciences (physics 1903, chemistry 1911).

Simulation: 100 radium nuclei. You can't predict when one nucleus will decay, but half of them always decay in the same time: the half-life. That's “le hasard qui devient une loi” (chance becomes a law).

8 · Chapter 4 · Au cœur de l'atome : le noyau

Rutherford (1909–1911) fires alpha particles at a very thin gold foil. Almost all go through, but a few bounce back, so the atom is mostly empty with a tiny positive nucleus, about 100,000 times smaller than the atom.

Simulation: compare Thomson's “plum pudding” model (no bounce-backs) with Rutherford's model (a few bounce back).

9 · Chapter 5 · Le Big Bang

Lemaître (1927) shows the Universe is expanding; Hubble (1929) sees galaxies moving away. Going back in time, the Universe was hot and dense. Hydrogen and helium formed in the first minutes; heavier elements (carbon, oxygen, iron) were made later inside stars. Age of the Universe: 13.8 billion years. Its “fossil light” (cosmic microwave background) was found by chance by Penzias & Wilson in 1965.

Experiment: the inflating balloon: dots all move apart, and no dot is the centre.

10 · The 3D model of the Universe

The Universe as a “trumpet”: time goes left → right, the width is the size of the Universe. It opens with a Big Bang explosion animation; the caption explains it's an artistic picture, since the Big Bang is not an explosion in space but space itself starting to grow.

  1. Le Big Bang: hot, dense state, 13.8 billion years ago
  2. L'inflation: a sudden, enormous growth in a fraction of a second
  3. La soupe de particules: quarks join into protons and neutrons
  4. Les premiers noyaux (3–20 min): ~75 % hydrogen, ~25 % helium
  5. Les premiers atomes (380,000 years): the Universe becomes transparent; the coloured disk is the fossil light
  6. Les âges sombres: no stars yet; gas gathers into filaments (the cosmic web); dark matter
  7. Les premières étoiles: they make carbon, oxygen, iron, then explode as supernovas
  8. Les galaxies: stars group into galaxies; the Milky Way forms
  9. Le Système solaire (4.6 billion years ago): we are made of star dust
  10. Aujourd'hui: the expansion is accelerating (dark energy); ordinary matter is only ~5 % of the Universe

Controls: drag to rotate, click a stage, or press “Lancer le voyage” for a guided tour (Pause / Reprendre continues where it stopped).

11 · Chapter 6 · Voir jusqu'aux atomes

Light can't show details smaller than ~0.2 µm. The electron microscope (Ruska & Knoll, 1931) uses electrons instead of light, so viruses become visible. The scanning tunnelling microscope (Binnig & Rohrer, 1981) follows a surface atom by atom with an ultra-fine tip, so atoms become visible. Nobel Prize 1986; in 1989 IBM wrote “IBM” with 35 xenon atoms.

Animation: the tip “reading atoms like braille”.

12 · Chapter 7 · La matière en apesanteur

Thomas Pesquet (2017): in the ISS, crystals of an anti-cancer drug grow more regular, because there's no sedimentation or convection without gravity. Sultan Al Neyadi (2023): a 3D bio-printer prints a human meniscus (knee cartilage) in space, where soft tissue doesn't collapse under its own weight.

Animation: crystals growing on Earth vs. in the ISS.

Key idea to say: this chapter links the three subjects: chemistry (crystals), biology (cells) and computing (3D printing).

14 · If you're asked how it was made (NSI)
